# EXPERIENCE_ARCHITECTURE

Project: KEIS Legal Group  
Branch: `premium-redesign-dorai`  
Status: customer experience architecture only  
Implementation: not approved, not started

## 0. Operating Principle

This document stops thinking in pages and blocks.

The product is designed around one person:

- they have just lost money;
- they are ashamed, angry, tired, and suspicious;
- they do not trust lawyers;
- they believe the money is gone;
- they expect to be sold another empty promise;
- they open the site looking for one thing: a reason not to give up.

The experience must not behave like a legal landing page.

It must behave like a calm, intelligent triage desk that turns panic into a case file.

Core experience thesis:

**KEIS helps a person move from chaos to claim.**

## 1. Repro: Current Experience Problem

Current structural diagnosis from the previous documents:

- The project has enough content, cases, reviews, forms, and visual material.
- The weak point is not lack of material.
- The weak point is sequence, emotional pacing, and decision design.
- The existing site pushes users through repeated page sections.
- A frightened user does not experience a human journey; they experience a service template.

Current emotional problem:

- The site asks for trust too early.
- It asks for contact too early.
- It repeats proof instead of building trust progressively.
- It treats panic as a conversion state, not as a fragile human state.

Required shift:

- from "show service";
- to "stabilize person";
- to "name what happened";
- to "show what can still be done";
- to "make contact feel rational, not desperate".

## 2. Primary Persona

### 2.1 Situation

The person has lost money in one of these states:

- transferred money to a broker or investment platform;
- transferred money under pressure;
- discovered a credit they did not consciously take;
- paid a seller, contractor, clinic, lawyer, bank, or insurer and received refusal;
- has already contacted bank/police/seller and heard a formal rejection.

### 2.2 Inner Monologue

Before opening the site:

- "I was stupid."
- "No one will return this."
- "Lawyers will take more money."
- "The bank already said it was my fault."
- "I do not understand what to do first."
- "I am tired of explaining this."
- "I need someone serious, but I do not know who to trust."

### 2.3 Behavioral State

- Skims, does not read deeply at first.
- Looks for signs that the firm understands the exact situation.
- Rejects generic promises.
- Needs proof before process.
- Needs a next action that feels small and safe.
- Is sensitive to pressure, urgency, and manipulative CTA language.

## 3. The First 3 Seconds

### What the person feels

Default emotional state:

- panic;
- shame;
- suspicion;
- fatigue;
- defensive attention;
- fear of being sold to.

### What the experience must do

In the first 3 seconds the site must not say:

- "We guarantee return";
- "Leave a request now";
- "Best lawyers";
- "30 years";
- "400 million";
- "Get consultation".

Those may be useful later, but first they can feel like pressure.

The first 3 seconds must communicate:

- "Your situation has a structure."
- "You are not the first person in this position."
- "There may still be traceable evidence."
- "We will not ask you to believe us before we show you how we think."

### First-screen emotional target

The person should feel:

- less alone;
- less chaotic;
- not blamed;
- invited to understand, not pushed to buy.

### First-screen memory

If the person closes the site after 3 seconds, they should remember:

**"They understand how these situations work."**

## 4. The First 10 Seconds

### What the person must understand

Within 10 seconds:

1. KEIS is not promising magic.
2. KEIS works through evidence, recipients, refusals, deadlines, and legal pressure.
3. The next step is not "pay a lawyer"; it is "structure what happened".
4. The person can begin without knowing legal terminology.

### What should be visible by 10 seconds

The experience should already expose:

- the main transformation: chaos -> evidence -> claim;
- a short statement that refusal is not always the end;
- a non-aggressive first action;
- one specific proof cue, not a wall of proof.

### Emotional target at 10 seconds

The person should feel:

- "Maybe this is not just a dead end."
- "They are not blaming me."
- "This looks more serious than a typical legal ad."

## 5. The First Minute

### What the person should feel

After about one minute:

- curiosity replaces panic;
- self-blame starts dropping;
- the situation feels nameable;
- legal action feels procedural, not mystical;
- trust begins to form through clarity, not persuasion.

### What the person should have learned

By one minute, the user should know:

- what type of situation they are in;
- what not to do next;
- what evidence matters;
- what the opponent will likely argue;
- why a formal refusal may not be final;
- what KEIS would check first.

### What the person should not yet be forced to do

They should not be forced to:

- fill a long form;
- choose a legal category;
- understand court terminology;
- read every service page;
- commit emotionally to "hiring lawyers".

## 6. Full Customer Journey

### Stage 1: Arrival

User state:
- panic, mistrust, shame, urgency.

User question:
- "Is this for my situation, or is this another empty legal site?"

Experience responsibility:
- stabilize;
- name the situation;
- avoid aggressive selling.

Required product behavior:
- one strong line;
- one visible transformation model;
- no clutter;
- no generic promise;
- no heavy form in the first breath.

Decision point:
- stay or bounce.

What must happen before the decision:
- the user must recognize their situation in plain language.

### Stage 2: Recognition

User state:
- "They are describing what happened to me."

User question:
- "Do they understand the specific mechanism, or are they just using broad words?"

Experience responsibility:
- show scenarios as lived events, not service names.

Required product behavior:
- diagnostic choices such as:
  - "I transferred money after pressure";
  - "I paid a broker and withdrawal is blocked";
  - "A credit appeared after account access";
  - "The seller or contractor refused";
  - "The clinic will not admit error";
  - "A bank/insurer says it is not their problem".

Decision point:
- choose a path or continue general explanation.

What must happen before the decision:
- the user must not need legal vocabulary.

### Stage 3: Reassurance

User state:
- shame starts to loosen, but suspicion remains.

User question:
- "Am I at fault? Will they judge me?"

Experience responsibility:
- remove blame without creating false hope.

Required product behavior:
- explain manipulative systems;
- show that confirmations, signatures, and transfers do not always tell the whole legal story;
- use calm expert language.

Decision point:
- emotionally permit themselves to keep reading.

What must happen before the decision:
- the user must feel seen, not sold to.

### Stage 4: Stop-Loss

User state:
- "What should I not do now?"

User question:
- "Can I make it worse?"

Experience responsibility:
- protect the user before converting them.

Required product behavior:
- Stop-Loss guidance:
  - do not pay new broker fees;
  - do not delete chats;
  - do not sign acceptance acts without comments;
  - do not rely only on phone calls;
  - do not miss refusal/deadline dates;
  - do not send more documents to suspicious actors.

Decision point:
- trust increases because the site gives protective value before asking for contact.

What must happen before the decision:
- the user must receive one useful action immediately.

### Stage 5: Evidence Orientation

User state:
- more rational, still anxious.

User question:
- "What do I have that matters?"

Experience responsibility:
- turn scattered facts into a manageable list.

Required product behavior:
- evidence strength model;
- plain categories:
  - payments;
  - chats;
  - calls;
  - contracts;
  - refusals;
  - statements;
  - screenshots;
  - medical records;
  - expert documents.

Decision point:
- "I have enough to ask them to look."

What must happen before the decision:
- evidence must feel recoverable, not overwhelming.

### Stage 6: Opponent Logic

User state:
- "But the bank/seller/clinic already refused."

User question:
- "Does refusal mean the case is over?"

Experience responsibility:
- decode the refusal.

Required product behavior:
- show what the opponent usually says;
- explain what that statement means;
- show what legal counter-evidence can answer it.

Decision point:
- user moves from hopelessness to possibility.

What must happen before the decision:
- the refusal must become an object of analysis, not a verdict.

### Stage 7: Proof Through Case File

User state:
- interested, needs proof.

User question:
- "Have they done this before in a similar situation?"

Experience responsibility:
- prove through narrative, not bragging.

Required product behavior:
- case file story:
  - what happened;
  - what the opponent argued;
  - what evidence mattered;
  - what legal move was made;
  - what result followed;
  - what this means for similar users.

Decision point:
- trust becomes active.

What must happen before the decision:
- case must be specific enough to feel real.

### Stage 8: Firm Trust

User state:
- "Maybe they know what they are doing. But can I trust them?"

User question:
- "Will they take my money and disappear?"

Experience responsibility:
- demonstrate standards, not just success.

Required product behavior:
- explain how KEIS decides whether to take a case;
- show what they check first;
- show when they may say the case is weak;
- show real contact and human accountability;
- show selected reviews as corroboration, not decoration.

Decision point:
- trust shifts from "they understand" to "they may be safe to contact".

What must happen before the decision:
- the user must see restraint and procedure.

### Stage 9: Low-Risk Contact

User state:
- ready but still protective.

User question:
- "What happens if I leave my phone?"

Experience responsibility:
- make contact feel like a rational next step.

Required product behavior:
- "Start a case file" rather than "Submit request";
- explain what happens after contact:
  - initial review;
  - what documents may be requested;
  - when a lawyer calls;
  - what will not happen without consent;
  - privacy note.

Decision point:
- leave request or save contact.

What must happen before the decision:
- the user must know the next 15 minutes, not just the legal future.

### Stage 10: Submission

User state:
- cautious relief.

User question:
- "Did I do the right thing?"

Experience responsibility:
- close the anxiety loop.

Required product behavior:
- confirmation;
- what to prepare;
- who will contact;
- expected time;
- alternative contact;
- reminder not to delete or pay/sign anything risky.

Decision point:
- wait calmly rather than continue searching competitors.

What must happen before the decision:
- the confirmation must reduce uncertainty.

## 7. Emotional Map

Primary emotional sequence:

Panic  
↓  
Recognition  
↓  
Permission to stop blaming oneself  
↓  
Curiosity  
↓  
Practical orientation  
↓  
First trust  
↓  
Hope  
↓  
Rational evaluation  
↓  
Controlled action  
↓  
Contact

### 7.1 Emotional States And Design Responsibilities

| Emotional state | User risk | Experience response | What to avoid |
|---|---|---|---|
| Panic | bounce or impulsive request | calm language, strong hierarchy | pressure, loud promises |
| Shame | hiding facts | normalize manipulative systems | blaming, "you should have" |
| Suspicion | rejects claims | show method before outcome | guarantees, stock trust |
| Curiosity | scans for fit | diagnostic scenarios | legal jargon |
| Hope | may become unrealistic | measured proof | overpromising |
| Rational decision | compares options | case file, standards, process | clutter |
| Contact readiness | fear of being sold | low-risk intake | long forms, vague next step |

## 8. Decision Points

### Decision Point 1: Stay Or Leave

When:
- first 3 seconds.

User asks:
- "Is this serious and relevant?"

Before decision, user needs:
- exact emotional recognition;
- no aggressive form;
- no generic legal claim.

Experience must show:
- "We understand how money-loss cases are structured."

### Decision Point 2: Choose A Situation

When:
- after initial recognition.

User asks:
- "Where do I fit?"

Before decision, user needs:
- plain-language scenarios;
- not legal taxonomy.

Experience must show:
- "Start with what happened, not what the law calls it."

### Decision Point 3: Continue Reading

When:
- after choosing or recognizing a situation.

User asks:
- "Is there any point?"

Before decision, user needs:
- one concrete reason hope exists:
  - traceable recipient;
  - formal refusal can be challenged;
  - evidence still exists;
  - deadline may still be open.

Experience must show:
- "This is not decided only by what the bank/seller/clinic said."

### Decision Point 4: Trust Method

When:
- after evidence and objection logic.

User asks:
- "Do they actually know what to do?"

Before decision, user needs:
- mechanism;
- case file proof;
- expert caution.

Experience must show:
- "We know the opponent's argument and the evidence needed to answer it."

### Decision Point 5: Trust Firm

When:
- after case proof.

User asks:
- "Are these people safe?"

Before decision, user needs:
- human accountability;
- standards;
- real contact;
- reviews;
- no manipulative urgency.

Experience must show:
- "We do not take trust for granted."

### Decision Point 6: Leave Request

When:
- after user sees next step.

User asks:
- "What happens if I submit?"

Before decision, user needs:
- clear next 15 minutes;
- privacy confidence;
- small first ask;
- low risk.

Experience must show:
- "This starts a review, not an obligation."

## 9. Trust Journey

Trust must not be a block.

Trust is a chain.

### Layer 1: Tone Trust

Built by:
- no blame;
- no panic language;
- no fake guarantees;
- measured confidence.

User conclusion:
- "They are not trying to scare me."

### Layer 2: Recognition Trust

Built by:
- describing the user's situation precisely;
- showing common manipulative patterns;
- using user language before legal language.

User conclusion:
- "They understand my situation."

### Layer 3: Method Trust

Built by:
- evidence model;
- opponent argument model;
- legal pressure map.

User conclusion:
- "They have a way to think about this."

### Layer 4: Proof Trust

Built by:
- case files;
- documents;
- outcomes;
- timelines;
- refusal/counterargument examples.

User conclusion:
- "They have handled similar matters."

### Layer 5: Restraint Trust

Built by:
- "we take the case when...";
- "weak case if...";
- "do not pay/sign/delete...";
- honest limits.

User conclusion:
- "They are not promising everything to everyone."

### Layer 6: Human Trust

Built by:
- counsel notes;
- team/work photography;
- office/contact;
- selected reviews;
- clear call process.

User conclusion:
- "There are accountable people behind this."

### Layer 7: Action Trust

Built by:
- low-risk case file start;
- clear next step;
- privacy note;
- confirmation guidance.

User conclusion:
- "Leaving a request is a reasonable next action."

## 10. Cognitive Load

### 10.1 Where The User Gets Tired

The user gets tired when:

- there are six equal cards with similar weight;
- they must choose between legal service names;
- proof metrics appear without context;
- FAQ repeats what was already said;
- every page repeats the same process;
- multiple CTAs ask the same thing;
- long explanatory text appears before emotional reassurance;
- case details are hidden too late;
- the page asks them to remember too many categories.

### 10.2 What Overloads The User

High-load patterns:

- "read all services";
- "understand legal taxonomy";
- "compare many equal cards";
- "fill form before understanding";
- "interpret generic review carousel";
- "process, cases, FAQ, reviews, CTA" as separate late sections.

### 10.3 What Must Be Removed To Reduce Load

Remove from the journey:

- duplicate metric bridges;
- generic repeated trust blocks;
- generic repeated reviews section on every page;
- long FAQ as default close;
- generic mid-page form;
- broad service grids without diagnostic language;
- any visual element that does not answer a user decision.

### 10.4 What Must Replace It

Replace with:

- one decision at a time;
- one dominant idea per screen;
- progressive disclosure;
- contextual objections;
- case-file proof;
- stop-loss guidance;
- low-risk intake.

## 11. Blocks That Should Disappear

These are "blocks" only because the source site thinks in blocks. In the new experience they should disappear as separate entities:

1. Generic "Частые проблемы" grids.
   - Why: they ask the user to scan symptoms before trust is built.

2. Generic "Выстраиваем честные отношения" block.
   - Why: trust must be demonstrated by the whole experience.

3. Duplicate metric bridge.
   - Why: metrics without narrative feel decorative.

4. Generic "Как идёт работа" repeated everywhere.
   - Why: process must be domain-specific.

5. Bottom-heavy FAQ.
   - Why: objections must appear where they matter.

6. Repeated review carousel.
   - Why: trust should be contextual, not generic social proof.

7. Generic mid-page CTA form.
   - Why: it interrupts the user's rational progression.

8. Route cards that only mirror navigation.
   - Why: routing must diagnose, not decorate.

9. Broad "Insights" strip on crisis pages.
   - Why: legal news can distract from urgent recovery.

10. Any parallax-only trust block.
   - Why: depth without decision support increases noise.

## 13. Blocks To Rethink Completely

### 13.1 Hero

Old role:
- announce service.

New role:
- stabilize and introduce transformation.

It must answer:
- "Am I safe here?"
- "Do they understand?"
- "Is there a method?"

### 13.2 Router

Old role:
- navigate services.

New role:
- help the user name what happened.

It should not ask:
- "Which service do you need?"

- "What happened first?"
- "What happened after refusal?"
- "What evidence do you still have?"

### 13.3 Trust

Old role:
- metrics, reviews, office.

New role:
- gradual proof of seriousness.

Trust must be distributed, not concentrated.

### 13.4 Cases

Old role:
- outcome cards.

New role:
- narrative proof of method.

Cases must show:
- conflict;
- evidence;
- objection;
- legal move;
- outcome.

### 13.5 CTA

Old role:
- collect lead.

- start a safe review.

- bottom bucket.

## 15. Speed Of Information Disclosure

### 0-3 seconds

Only:
- recognition;
- calm;
- method hint.

No:
- heavy metrics;
- long forms;
- full service grid;
- legal jargon.

### 3-10 seconds

Reveal:
- transformation model;
- "what happened" language;
- first non-sales action.

No:
- full process;
- all cases;
- all directions.

### 10-30 seconds

Reveal:
- stop-loss guidance;
- diagnostic route;
- first evidence categories.

No:
- long text;
- repeated proof;
- generic FAQ.

### 30-60 seconds

Reveal:
- refusal decoder;
- opponent logic;
- case file preview.

No:
- detailed legal doctrine unless needed.

### 60-120 seconds

Reveal:
- deeper case file;
- standards;
- human trust;
- low-risk contact.

No:
- pressure to decide before trust is built.

### After 120 seconds

Reveal:
- detailed FAQ;
- journal links;
- policy;
- more reviews;
- deeper practice content.

This is where secondary content belongs, not before the person has stabilized.

## 17. Why This Experience Is Better Than Competitors

Most competitors:

- lead with fear;
- show generic lawyer photos;
- promise results too early;
- ask for contact before proof;
- rely on review blocks and metrics;
- use service taxonomy;
- hide legal method;
- make users feel like leads.

This experience:

- protects before selling;
- removes shame;
- shows method before claims;
- treats refusal as analyzable evidence;
- makes legal work visible;
- gives users a low-risk first step;
- builds trust progressively;
- turns the site into a decision tool, not an ad.

Competitive advantage:

- competitors say "we can help";
- KEIS should show "this is how your case becomes actionable".
