## 0. Repro

Base documents:

- `DESIGN_RESTRUCTURE_PLAN.md`
- `DESIGN_RESTRUCTURE_REVIEW.md`
- `EXPERIENCE_ARCHITECTURE.md`

Current conclusion:

- The product should not look like a legal landing page.
- The product should not look like a premium wrapper around old sections.
- The product should visually express the experience thesis: **from chaos to claim**.
- The strongest visual metaphor is not "law firm", but **case file becoming structured**.

This document defines visual rules only. It does not define implementation, components, HTML, CSS, JS, or Figma layouts.

## 1. Visual Philosophy

### 1.1 Desired Impression

The site should feel like:

- an international legal advisory firm;
- a calm crisis desk;
- a precise evidence lab;
- a private case-file workspace;
- a serious institutional practice;
- a place where chaos is processed into legal structure.

The site must feel:

- expensive, but not decorative;
- calm, but not passive;
- intellectual, but not coldly academic;
- strategic, but not corporate-empty;
- human, but not sentimental;
- high-trust, but not dependent on badges;
- modern, but not startup-playful;
- Russian-market relevant, but internationally composed.

### 1.2 What The Visual Language Must Communicate

In one glance:

- "This firm understands complexity."
- "This is not a panic funnel."
- "Evidence matters here."
- "There is a structured path."
- "You are not being sold a miracle."

### 1.3 What It Must Never Communicate

Never feel like:

- a mass-market legal leadgen site;
- a debt collector or claims factory;
- a bank dispute call center;
- a luxury brand pretending to be legal;
- a template with dark hero and orange CTA;
- a page made from unrelated cards;
- a fear-based fraud recovery ad.

## 2. Brand Personality

Primary traits:

- Authoritative.
- Calm.
- Rational.
- Strategic.
- Precise.
- Premium.
- Reserved.
- Evidence-led.
- Humanly composed.
- Institutionally serious.

Secondary traits:

- Protective.
- Forensic.
- Methodical.
- Literate.
- Modern.
- Restrained.
- Deliberate.
- Direct.

What the brand is not:

- loud;
- heroic;
- theatrical;
- sentimental;
- flashy;
- urgent for the sake of urgency;
- aggressive;
- glossy;
- overly warm;
- bureaucratic.

Brand voice translated into visuals:

- Do not shout.
- Do not decorate.
- Do not over-explain with equal-weight blocks.
- Show sequence, hierarchy, and judgement.
- Make the user feel the matter is being handled by adults.

## 3. Typography Direction

No specific fonts are selected here. This defines behavior.

### 3.1 Headline Scale

Hero headlines:

- large enough to feel editorial and institutional;
- not so large that the page becomes a campaign poster;
- should hold one exact thought, not a paragraph;
- should usually be 1-2 lines on desktop when possible;
- may allow 3 lines only when the legal idea needs tension.

Section headlines:

- materially smaller than hero;
- direct and declarative;
- no marketing slogans;
- should be understood in 2 seconds;
- should not rely on decorative line breaks.

Card headlines:

- compact;
- precise;
- not dramatic;
- readable while scanning;
- should not exceed the visual weight of the card's evidence or result.

### 3.2 Density

The site should feel editorial, not sparse to the point of emptiness.

Text density rules:

- first screen: very low density;
- recognition and stop-loss moments: low density;
- evidence and case-file areas: medium density;
- legal appendix/FAQ areas: high density allowed only after trust is built.

The user must never encounter dense legal text before emotional stabilization.

### 3.3 Line Height And Rhythm

Typography should breathe, but not float.

Principles:

- headlines need controlled line height, not airy poster spacing;
- body copy should feel calm and legible;
- legal notes can be tighter, but never cramped;
- captions and evidence labels should feel exact, like case annotations;
- avoid ornamental typography effects.

### 3.4 Contrast

Use contrast to express hierarchy:

- hero statement vs proof detail;
- fact vs interpretation;
- evidence vs claim;
- opponent argument vs KEIS response;
- user situation vs legal next step.

Do not create contrast only through color. Use scale, weight, spacing, alignment, and position.

### 3.5 Line Length

Recommended behavior:

- hero: short, controlled lines;
- body: readable editorial measure;
- cards: short fragments;
- evidence annotations: very short lines;
- case narratives: moderate lines with clear breaks;
- legal policy/FAQ: longer measure allowed only in secondary areas.

No screen should ask the user to read more than one primary idea at once.

### 3.6 Amount Of Text Per Screen

Information pacing:

- 1 second: one phrase and one visual structure.
- 5 seconds: one claim and one proof cue.
- 15 seconds: one decision and one next action.
- 60 seconds: one complete logic loop.

If a screen contains:

- more than one decision;
- more than one CTA;
- more than one dominant proof;
- more than one emotional job;

then the screen is overloaded.

## 4. Layout Principles

### 4.1 Page Construction

Pages should be built like a case file unfolding, not like stacked marketing sections.

Base sequence of visual behavior:

1. Wide calm field.
2. One controlling statement.
3. Evidence begins to appear.
4. The user's situation is named.
5. Structure forms.
6. Proof becomes specific.
7. Action becomes safe.

### 4.2 Air

Use generous air around:

- first-screen idea;
- case-file transformations;
- counsel notes;
- critical stop-loss warnings;
- final low-risk intake.

Use tighter rhythm around:

- evidence lists;
- legal annotations;
- document anatomy;
- refusal decoder;
- procedural clocks.

Air must mean importance, not luxury decoration.

### 4.3 Containers

Container principles:

- main content should feel centered and controlled;
- evidence surfaces may break the standard content width when they need to feel operational;
- legal notes should be narrower for authority and readability;
- case-file surfaces can be wider to imply workspace;
- avoid full-width everything.

The container system must create a feeling of jurisdiction: every object has a place.

### 4.4 Vertical Rhythm

The rhythm should alternate between:

- calm editorial space;
- dense evidence surface;
- calm interpretation;
- proof narrative;
- action.

Do not stack dense blocks. After every dense reasoning area, give the user an interpretive pause.

### 4.5 Section Distance

Sections should not feel like separate cards floating in a page.

Transitions should feel like progression:

- from issue to evidence;
- from evidence to objection;
- from objection to legal move;
- from legal move to contact.

Use distance to mark changes in cognitive mode, not just visual separation.

### 4.6 Hero Scale

Hero must feel decisive, not oversized.

Hero rules:

- one primary statement;
- one proof model or visual case-file surface;
- no long form as the main visual object;
- no generic lawyer/team collage;
- no heavy statistics before relevance is established;
- no more than one dominant action.

### 4.7 Image Scale

Images should be large when they establish:

- institutional presence;
- real work environment;
- evidence materiality;
- case-file depth.

Images should be small or annotated when they serve:

- document proof;
- process state;
- case detail;
- expert note.

No image should exist just to fill a layout.

## 5. Image Language

### 5.1 Image Roles

Use images as:

- proof of real environment;
- evidence material;
- legal atmosphere;
- case-file artifact;
- human accountability;
- editorial context.

Do not use images as:

- generic mood;
- decoration;
- filler;
- stock-like emotion;
- fake urgency;
- dark danger wallpaper.

### 5.2 Allowed Image Categories

Allowed:

- real KEIS office and team;
- hands reviewing documents;
- case folders and annotated documents;
- court/legal preparation details;
- evidence still-life;
- receipts, statements, contracts, refusal letters;
- medical/consumer/construction documents when staged carefully;
- city/office context used sparingly;
- editorial news imagery if curated.

### 5.3 Forbidden Image Categories

Forbidden:

- smiling generic lawyers shaking hands;
- abstract justice scales as primary visuals;
- gavels as hero imagery;
- anonymous people looking stressed at laptops;
- dark hacker/crypto cliche visuals;
- money flying, broken phones, warning triangles as decoration;
- AI-generated people in trust-critical areas;
- decorative city skylines without relation to KEIS;
- overly blurred atmospheric photos;
- luxury textures with no legal function.

### 5.4 Image Treatment

Treatment principles:

- documentary, calm, low-drama;
- natural contrast, not glossy;
- restrained color temperature;
- no aggressive vignettes;
- no heavy blur;
- no fake cinematic darkness;
- no stock-style overprocessing.

Images should feel:

- inspected;
- selected;
- part of a file;
- legally relevant.

### 5.5 Framing

Preferred framing:

- 3/4 desk views with documents;
- cropped document details with annotations;
- side-light office scenes;
- hands and materials, not posed smiles;
- architectural office details used as trust background;
- case evidence photographed top-down or at controlled angle.

Avoid:

- centered stock portrait compositions;
- overly wide empty conference room shots;
- dramatic low angles;
- cluttered desks;
- fake court drama.

### 5.6 Existing Assets Guidance

Use existing assets only if they can support the case-file language:

- office/team images: use for institutional and human trust;
- case documents: use for proof;
- service images: use if they can be reframed as evidence context;
- generated atmospheric images: use only as secondary support, never as first trust proof;
- news covers: use only after editorial curation.

## 6. Cards

Cards must not feel like generic UI cards.

They should feel like:

- archive fragments;
- case-file entries;
- evidence records;
- annotated material;
- legal observations;
- decision objects.

### 6.1 Card Families

#### Evidence Card

Feeling:
- a document entered into a case file.

Should communicate:
- source;
- relevance;
- strength;
- missing link;
- next use.

#### Refusal Card

Feeling:
- a formal refusal decoded by a lawyer.

Should communicate:
- what they said;
- what it means;
- why it may not end the case;
- what to check.

#### Case File Card

Feeling:
- a condensed legal narrative.

Should communicate:
- situation;
- opponent argument;
- evidence;
- legal move;
- result.

#### Stop-Loss Card

Feeling:
- immediate protective instruction.

Should communicate:
- do not do this;
- why;
- what to preserve.

#### Counsel Note

Feeling:
- expert margin note.

Should communicate:
- judgement;
- caution;
- next thought.

#### Route Card

Feeling:
- diagnostic entry point, not navigation tile.

Should communicate:
- "this happened to me";
- likely category;
- first evidence;
- first safe action.

### 6.2 Card Rules

Cards should:

- be readable in 3 seconds;
- have one reason to exist;
- reveal hierarchy instantly;
- feel tactile but not skeuomorphic;
- use labels like a file system, not decorative tags;
- avoid equal visual weight when importance differs;
- never become a grid of interchangeable marketing claims.

### 6.3 What Cards Must Never Be

Cards must never be:

- ornamental boxes;
- equal six-item grids by default;
- containers for vague benefits;
- mini landing pages;
- colorful badges;
- hover toys;
- a substitute for real hierarchy.

## 7. Motion Language

Motion must express thinking, not entertainment.

### 7.1 Motion Personality

Motion should feel:

- calm;
- deliberate;
- precise;
- almost procedural;
- intelligent;
- weighty without being slow.

Motion should never feel:

- playful;
- springy;
- flashy;
- nervous;
- gamified;
- decorative;
- distracting.

### 7.2 What Animates

Animate:

- evidence aligning into sequence;
- document layers revealing hierarchy;
- refusal text being decoded into legal meaning;
- case file states changing;
- header compacting on scroll;
- cards revealing secondary evidence on intent;
- CTA confirming readiness;
- progress from chaos to claim.

### 7.3 What Never Animates

Never animate:

- legal disclaimers;
- consent text;
- phone/contact clarity;
- critical warnings in a way that delays reading;
- user input fields for decoration;
- trust proof just to impress;
- reviews in a way that hides access;
- anything that could make the page feel unstable.

### 7.4 Speed

Speed principles:

- immediate feedback for hover/click;
- slower transitions for case-file assembly;
- very restrained parallax;
- no motion that makes the user wait for information;
- no chained animation before essential text appears.

Motion should support comprehension before delight.

### 7.5 Parallax

Use parallax only when it expresses depth of evidence or institutional space.

Allowed:

- document layer shift;
- case file depth;
- office/space depth;
- subtle background separation.

Forbidden:

- decorative floating layers;
- heavy hero parallax;
- motion that makes text harder to read;
- atmospheric movement without decision value.

### 7.6 Header

Header behavior:

- calm at first;
- compact after scroll;
- never visually aggressive;
- should communicate stability;
- mega-menu should feel like an organized practice map;
- current section state should help orientation.

The header should not compete with the user's crisis.

### 7.7 Hover

Hover should reveal:

- evidence detail;
- next action;
- relationship;
- status;
- not decoration.

Hover should not:

- lift every card equally;
- use dramatic glow;
- cause layout shift;
- hide essential information from non-hover users.

## 8. Color Philosophy

No palette is selected here. This defines color logic.

### 8.1 Base World

The product should live mostly in:

- controlled light surfaces;
- legal paper neutrals;
- deep ink;
- institutional darks;
- restrained evidence accents.

The base should feel:

- serious;
- legible;
- calm;
- archival;
- modern.

### 8.2 Accent Use

Accent color is not decoration.

Use accent only for:

- current decision;
- evidence highlight;
- legal state;
- active path;
- selected case-file item;
- primary CTA;
- important warning.

If everything is accented, nothing is important.

### 8.3 Gold / Warm Accent

Gold or warm metallic tones may be used only when:

- expressing premium restraint;
- highlighting institutional confidence;
- marking a successful legal outcome;
- adding warmth to otherwise cold evidence surfaces.

Never use gold for:

- fake luxury;
- borders everywhere;
- decorative gradients;
- button addiction;
- "expensive" signaling without function.

### 8.4 Dark Background

Dark backgrounds should be rare.

Use dark when:

- the page needs a serious reset;
- showing crisis, risk, or high-stakes legal pressure;
- creating a case-file workspace;
- separating a major proof narrative.

Do not use dark:

- by default in every hero;
- to hide weak imagery;
- to create generic premium mood;
- behind long body text without reason.

### 8.5 Light Background

Light backgrounds should carry most of the experience.

Use light for:

- clarity;
- trust;
- evidence reading;
- document interpretation;
- refusal decoding;
- contact confidence.

The experience should not feel gloomy. The user arrives in fear; the product should bring light and structure.

### 8.6 Warning Color

Warning color should be used only for actual user-protection moments:

- do not pay again;
- deadline risk;
- do not delete evidence;
- do not sign;
- do not ignore credit history.

Never use warning color to manufacture urgency.

## 9. Trust System

Trust must be visual before it is verbal.

### 9.1 Visual Trust Signals

Trust is built visually through:

- controlled hierarchy;
- stable spacing;
- real materials;
- document-level detail;
- restraint;
- evidence sequencing;
- human accountability;
- expert notes;
- no visual desperation;
- clear next steps.

### 9.2 What Builds Trust Without Text

Visual trust should come from:

- real office photography;
- real document textures or credible staged evidence;
- structured case-file surfaces;
- consistent labels;
- precise alignment;
- stable header behavior;
- no clutter around CTAs;
- balanced whitespace;
- serious but readable typography;
- restrained image treatment.

### 9.3 What Destroys Trust

Trust is weakened by:

- too many CTAs;
- stock legal imagery;
- decorative icons;
- overuse of gradients;
- overuse of dark mood;
- cards with vague promises;
- huge metrics without context;
- animated gimmicks;
- inconsistent image sources;
- loud success language.

### 9.4 Distributed Trust

Trust should appear as a chain:

1. tone;
2. recognition;
3. method;
4. evidence;
5. restraint;
6. human accountability;
7. safe action.

No single trust block can carry the product.

## 10. Visual Hierarchy

### 10.1 After 1 Second

The person should see:

- one serious statement;
- calm visual field;
- evidence/case-file cue;
- no aggressive sales pressure.

They should think:

- "This is different from a legal ad."

### 10.2 After 5 Seconds

The person should see:

- the transformation model;
- one relevant scenario or proof cue;
- a sense of order forming.

They should think:

- "They understand the kind of problem I have."

### 10.3 After 15 Seconds

The person should see:

- what not to do next;
- what evidence matters;
- why the situation may not be over.

They should think:

- "There is something practical I can do."

### 10.4 After 1 Minute

The person should see:

- a structured case-file logic;
- specific proof;
- opponent/refusal logic;
- human/legal standards;
- a low-risk next action.

They should think:

- "Leaving a request is a rational next step, not a desperate one."

## 12. UI Principles

These are design-behavior principles, not implementation rules.

### 12.1 One Screen, One Job

Every screen should have one primary user job:

- recognize;
- understand;
- preserve;
- decode;
- trust;
- decide;
- contact.

If a screen does two of these equally, it is not resolved.

### 12.2 Interface As Legal Thinking

The UI should look like the firm thinks:

- ordered;
- precise;
- evidence-led;
- calm under pressure.

The interface itself must create confidence before the copy explains it.

### 12.3 Progressive Disclosure

Do not reveal the full legal world at once.

Reveal:

1. emotional recognition;
2. immediate protection;
3. evidence;
4. objection;
5. proof;
6. standards;
7. contact.

### 12.4 Status Over Decoration

Visual marks should communicate state:

- strong evidence;
- missing evidence;
- urgent;
- refusal;
- next step;
- reviewed;
- risky.

No decorative badges.

### 12.5 Human But Not Soft

The product must reduce shame, but it should not become soft or sentimental.

Humanity comes from:

- restraint;
- clarity;
- expert caution;
- non-blaming language;
- real people behind the process.

## 13. Design Rules

1. Do not use more than one main accent per screen.
2. Do not use decorative elements without a decision function.
3. Every screen must have one job.
4. Every block must answer one user question.
5. Do not show more than one primary CTA on a screen.
6. Images must strengthen meaning, not fill space.
7. A card must be readable in 3 seconds.
8. No six-card grid unless the six items truly have equal decision weight.
9. Do not use metrics before relevance is established.
10. Do not use trust badges as a substitute for method.
11. Do not use dark background as default premium mood.
12. Do not use gold unless it marks restraint, value, or outcome.
13. Do not animate anything that delays comprehension.
14. Do not animate legal warnings for decoration.
15. Do not place long legal text before emotional stabilization.
16. Do not ask users to choose legal categories before naming their situation.
17. Do not use generic lawyer imagery.
18. Do not use gavels, scales, warning triangles, or hacker cliches as primary visuals.
19. Do not make CTA feel like a sales trap.
20. Do not let forms dominate the first screen.
21. Use whitespace to create authority, not emptiness.
22. Use density only where the user is ready for analysis.
23. Use document-like surfaces only when they carry real information.
24. Every visual label must clarify status or meaning.
25. Every hover state must reveal useful information or confirm intent.
26. Every case must show conflict, evidence, legal move, and result.
27. Every refusal must be decoded, not merely displayed.
28. Every warning must protect the user, not manipulate them.
29. Every page family must have a distinct emotional rhythm.
30. Every repeated pattern must be questioned before reuse.
31. Typography must create hierarchy before color does.
32. Body copy must never form a wall in the first minute.
33. Reviews must be contextual, not generic decoration.
34. Office/team imagery must feel real and accountable.
35. Journal cards must show practical consequence, not just article titles.
36. The header must orient, not advertise.
37. The footer must close trust, not become a dumping ground.
38. Motion must show structure forming.
39. The user must always know what happens next.
40. The product must visually say: "your situation can be structured."

## 14. Approval Assessment

### Is the product ready to move to Figma?

Not fully.

It is closer, but one layer is still missing before Figma:

**content-to-experience mapping.**

The team should not open Figma until the following are defined:

1. First-screen message for the primary fraud journey.
2. Exact case-file data model.
3. Exact evidence categories per page family.
4. Exact refusal decoder examples.
5. Which real assets can support trust-critical moments.
6. Which pages get stop-loss alerts and what they say.
7. Which proof metrics are legally and factually safe to use.
8. Which reviews match which page family.
9. Which visual surfaces are global and which are page-specific.
10. Which content is allowed in the first minute and which is delayed.

### Conditional Figma Gate

The product can move to Figma only after approval of:

- visual thesis: "case file becoming structured";
- image restrictions;
- card families;
- motion principles;
- one-screen-one-job rule;
- reference matrix;
- 40 design rules;
- content-to-experience mapping as the next documentation step.

Until then, implementation and interface drawing should not begin.
